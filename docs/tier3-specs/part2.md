**`incident-response` · Incident Response & On-Call**
Track `ops` · *"Mitigate first, understand second"* · Builds on: `observability`
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · The page (scenario).** A `ScenarioStory` over one invented incident: checkout errors begin at minute 0 (a deploy finishes at 14:02), a symptom alert fires at minute 4, the on-call acknowledges at minute 6. The learner plays the on-call engineer. A **cause picker** (`Segmented`: *bad deploy*, *slow dependency*, *one region down*) sets what is really wrong. The learner does not see it until they investigate or mitigate, and the story replays against each cause so every ending is reachable (AC-0/T3 #24).
- **Decisions, in order.**
  1. Declare a severity from an *example* scale (SEV1/2/3, defined on screen as one organisation's example; real scales differ) and assign roles from chips: **incident commander** (decides, does not debug), **operations lead** (hands on keyboard), **communications lead** (status updates), **scribe** (timeline). Skipping roles is allowed and costs something visible: no comms lead means the status update is late (trust meter); no commander means two responders act at once (a conflicting-actions delay, stated in the ledger).
  2. Pick the first action: **roll back the last deploy** (4 min to take effect), **fail over to the standby region** (7 min), **switch off the optional recommendations feature with a flag** (2 min, partial), or **investigate** (3 min, reveals the cause).
  3. After a mitigation that did nothing for this cause, the story offers the next choice. Nothing is a dead end (`validateScenario`).
- **The model (`model.ts`, pure).** The impact rate is failed requests per minute: 400 of 1,000 until a mitigation that works for that cause takes effect. `ACTION_EFFECT[cause][action]` gives `{ minutes, rateAfter }`:
  - rollback fixes *bad deploy* (rate 0 at +4 min) and does nothing for the other two;
  - failover fixes *region down* (+7 min) and does nothing for *bad deploy*, because the standby runs the same build;
  - the flag cuts *slow dependency* from 400 to 120 per minute (+2 min): a degraded mitigation, not a fix;
  - investigate costs 3 min and sets `knownCause`.
  Total impact is the sum of rate x minutes between events. With the default numbers, an immediate rollback on *bad deploy* mitigates at minute 10 for 4,000 failed requests; investigate-then-rollback is minute 13 for 5,200; failover-then-rollback is minute 17 for 6,800 (the test recomputes all three). Meters: **failed requests so far**, **minutes since onset**, **customer trust** (0-100, drops 5 per 10 minutes without a status update; each update restores part of it). Every constant is named and illustrative.
- **Scene 2 · Timeline replay.** After the ending, a `GanttLanes` replay (minutes axis) with lanes *detect, acknowledge, mitigate, resolve, comms*, zero-length ticks (K3-11) for *alert fired*, *page acked*, *mitigation started*, *mitigation took effect*, drawn progressively with `barAt` (K3-10), beside a `LogStream` (K3-9) of invented log lines that includes the 14:02 deploy marker a quick look would have shown. A readout gives **time to detect, acknowledge, mitigate and resolve** with the definition used here (definitions vary by organisation; the lesson says so).
- **Scene 3 · The outcome table.** Every first-action x cause outcome, computed by enumerating the scenario graph and sortable with `SortTh` (K3-4): time to mitigate and failed requests, with the learner's own path marked. It shows that *mitigate first* does not mean *act blind*: a cheap, reversible action matched to the evidence beats both a long investigation and a wrong guess, and the wrong-guess cost is in the table.
**Out of scope.** Post-incident analysis (`postmortems`), alert quality (`alerting-design`), SLO and burn-rate arithmetic (`slos-error-budgets`), real tools (paging products, runbook products), on-call rota design beyond one sentence on sustainability.
**Kit.** `ScenarioStory` + `validateScenario` (K3-15), `GanttLanes` with `barAt` and instants (K3-10, K3-11), `LogStream` (K3-9), `SortTh` / `useSortedRows` (K3-4), `Segmented` (cause), `ChipButton` (roles), `Stat` (K3-3 multi-line sub), `Bar` (trust), `Provenance` (K3-7).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** failed requests and the impact curve `--bad`; a mitigation that worked `--ok`; one that did nothing for this cause `--orange` (a second kind of miss, not a system failure); the action in progress `--hi`; the page and in-flight actions `--info`; roles `--violet` chips; choices not yet available `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "the on-call should find the root cause before changing anything", and its mirror, "just try things until it stops". A quiz question shows an invented timeline where the last deploy is 2 minutes before the errors and a rollback is cheap and reversible, and asks what to do first.
- **Correctness:**
  - Engine totals equal an **independent minute-by-minute loop** (sum the rate each minute) for every path the scenario graph allows, for all three causes (bounded enumeration).
  - `validateScenario` passes on the real scenario and **fails on a broken copy** (a node with no way out, a meter leaving 0-100).
  - The outcome table equals the enumeration's minimum per cause.
  - Rollback leaves *slow dependency* unfixed and failover leaves *bad deploy* unfixed (named tests); a test walks to every ending.
- **Hostile input:** no free-text field. Replay controls clamp to the timeline; a double-click on a choice records one decision.
- **Honesty:** `Provenance`: "invented incident, illustrative numbers", plus the K3-15 mandatory scenario line. "SEV levels and role names are examples; the incident-command structure is borrowed from emergency services and widely used (**common practice**; verify against a public SRE incident-management chapter before quoting a source)". *Mitigate first* is labelled **common practice**, not a law.
**Phone.** Choices stack as a radio group; the Gantt replay sits in a `WideRegion minWidth={480}`; the log stream wraps. Look at first, middle and last frame of each scene at 390 and 768 (AC-0/T3 #26).

**`postmortems` · Blameless Postmortems & Contributing Factors**
Track `ops` · *"Systems fail; ask why, not who"* · Builds on: `incident-response`
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · The factors tree.** An invented incident (restated in two lines so the lesson stands alone): a config change took checkout down for 27 minutes. A `TreeSvg` starts with the *impact* at the root. Clicking a node asks "what made this possible?" and reveals 1-3 child factors from a fixed authored tree (about 14 nodes, three levels). Each is tagged **trigger**, **contributing** or **latent condition** (the tag explains itself on click). There is no single "root cause": the tree has several leaves and the lesson counts them.
- **Scene 2 · Blame to system.** Eight authored sentences ("Dana pushed the bad config", "the engineer should have known"), each with three rewrite chips. The learner picks the rewrite that names a condition instead of a person ("the config tool accepted a value outside the valid range"). A wrong pick says what the rewrite still blames. Names are fictional and labelled.
- **Scene 3 · Action items.** The learner builds each item from slots: *what*, *owner role* (not a person), *done when*, and tags it **prevent**, **detect** or **mitigate**. A checklist computed from the data says: every leaf factor has at least one action; every action has a tag, an owner role and a verifiable done-when; the **mix** of tags (a postmortem of only "prevent" items, nothing that would have detected the problem sooner or shortened it, is flagged). Presets: *a good postmortem*; *"be more careful"* (one item, no owner, no measure: fails every check); *a chain of whys that ends at a person*.
- **Scene 4 · Five Whys vs a tree.** The same facts walked as one chain (it stops at the first convenient cause) and as the tree; a counter shows how many factors each surfaced.
**Out of scope.** Incident handling (`incident-response`), burn-rate policy (`slos-error-budgets`), blameless culture as management (Tier 4 lead lessons), legal and compliance writing.
**Kit.** `TreeSvg` (`nodeMark`), `ChipButton` / `Segmented` (rewrites, tags), `CodePanel` (the action-item sentence with `stateOf` good/bad), `Tray`, `Provenance`, `useShake`. The tree is explored, not decided, so this is data in `TreeSvg`, not a `ScenarioStory`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the node being asked about `--hi`; a revealed factor `--info`; **trigger** `--orange`, **contributing** `--violet`, **latent condition** `--amber` (categories, never `--bad`); an action item that passes `--ok`, one that fails `--bad`; a leaf with no action gets a `--bad` ring. Action tags are letter chips **P / D / M** in `muted`, so colour is never the only cue.
- **Misconception:** "a postmortem finds the root cause and who caused it", and "a good action item is *be more careful*". A quiz question shows two action items and asks which is a real fix.
- **Correctness:**
  - Data tests on the authored tree: acyclic, every node reachable from the root, every leaf tagged.
  - The checklist is computed by an **independent walk** (not the tree-building code) over the same data, and the three presets are pinned: good passes all; "be more careful" fails owner, measure and coverage; the chain surfaces fewer factors than the tree by a number computed from the data.
  - Every rewrite chip has a reason string; each blame sentence has exactly one correct rewrite.
- **Hostile input:** slots are chips or bounded text (max 80 characters); an empty slot marks the item incomplete and never passes it; text renders as text.
- **Honesty:** "invented incident, invented names". *Blameless* and *several contributing factors rather than one root cause* are labelled **common practice** and **one view** (a human-factors tradition; verify against the incident-analysis literature rather than quoting a book from memory).
**Phone.** The tree at 356 px with a `minWidth` from `minWidthForText(W, 11)`; rewrite and slot controls stack.

**`alerting-design` · Alerting: Symptoms vs Causes & Alert Fatigue**
Track `ops` · *"Page on pain, not noise"* · Builds on: `slos-error-budgets`
**Merge test (plan §1.2).** The topic ships because it has three manipulables beyond a threshold slider: (a) a rule editor whose pages, false pages and misses are *computed* over a seeded week, (b) a triage board that routes twelve alerts to page, ticket or dashboard and totals a human cost, and (c) a lead-time scene where a cause-style alert is the right ticket. If the builder finds scene (a) alone carries the lesson, it reports to the coordinator for a merge into `incident-response` instead of shipping a thin one.
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · A seeded week.** One week at one-minute resolution of two series from a seeded generator (K3-2 `createTickSim` or a pure function): **CPU %** (noisy, with 20 batch-job spikes to 85-95% that harm nobody) and **checkout error ratio** (baseline 0.1%, **three real incidents** at known windows: 40 min at 5%, 12 min at 30%, 90 min at 1.5%, plus 15 one-minute blips of 8% that are noise). Incident windows are drawn as bands, labelled "authored for this lesson".
- **Rule editor.** Pick the metric, a threshold slider and a **for-duration** slider ("must hold N consecutive minutes", 0-30). A page fires when the condition has held for N minutes and stays firing until it clears; each firing episode is one page. Computed readouts: **pages per week**, **pages during a real incident** (useful), **pages with no incident** (false), **incidents never paged** (missed), **minutes from incident start to first page** per incident. Extremes are stated truthfully: a threshold below the baseline is always firing, and the caption says "never clears", not "keeps paging".
- **Scene 2 · Symptom vs cause.** Cause alerts (CPU above 80%, disk above 70%, a pod restart) beside the symptom alert (error ratio above the threshold for N minutes). Cause alerts page constantly while users are fine; the symptom alert pages in incidents. A toggle adds the one honest cause alert, a **disk-full prediction** ("fills in under 4 hours", a linear extrapolation of the lesson's own series) routed to a **ticket**: it has lead time and nobody is hurt yet. (Real monitoring systems have such a function; the lesson names the idea, not a product's syntax.)
- **Scene 3 · Triage board.** Twelve authored alerts ("CPU high on batch-7", "error ratio high on checkout", "certificate expires in 6 days"). Route each to **page**, **ticket** or **dashboard only**; the lesson scores against a stated rubric (*does a human need to act now? is there a clear action? is a user hurt now?*), totals the night-time pages the routing would cause at an assumed share of 02:00 firings, and flags each paging alert with no runbook link.
**Out of scope.** Burn-rate and multi-window alert maths (`slos-error-budgets` owns them; this lesson says "use the burn-rate alert from there" and does not recompute it), incident roles (`incident-response`), alert-manager products and their configuration syntax.
**Kit.** `Plot2D` (series; incident bands via `Segments` or `Area` from K3-1), `Slider` with `format`, `MetricMeter` (K3-13: pages per week, bands), `Stat` (K3-3), `ChipButton` and Move buttons for triage (keyboard path required), `Segmented`, `Provenance`, `useTickSim` (K3-2) only if the week plays live.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** CPU series `--info`, error ratio `--hi`; incident bands `--bad` tint; a useful page `--ok`, a false page `--orange`, a missed incident `--bad` outline; the threshold line `--violet`; routed alerts `--info`.
- **Misconception:** "more alerts mean safer", and "alert when CPU is high". A quiz question gives a rule that fired 61 times last week with no incident and asks what to do with it.
- **Correctness:**
  - Pages, false pages, misses and time-to-page come from a **run-length implementation**, checked against an **independent per-minute state machine** over the whole threshold x duration grid, and against hand-worked windows (a one-minute 8% blip pages at for-duration 0 and does not at 2).
  - Extremes pinned: threshold 0 gives one never-clearing page; threshold 100% gives zero pages and three misses.
  - Caption sentences come from the same numbers (AC-0/T3 #14), tested at the slider extremes.
- **Hostile input:** sliders clamp; no typed rules, so no parser.
- **Honesty:** "synthetic week, seeded (seed shown), authored incidents"; "thresholds are illustrative, not recommendations"; the rubric is labelled **common practice**.
**Phone.** The week plot at 356 px with the bands readable; readouts stack; triage is a vertical list with Move buttons.

**`safe-config-rollout` · Config Changes as Deployments**
Track `ops` · *"The outage was a one-line config"* · Builds on: `feature-flags`
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · Everything at once.** A fleet of 48 servers in 4 regions of 12 (a `CellGrid`, one cell per server, rows are regions). The learner picks one of four authored changes and pushes it fleet-wide:
  1. **fails validation** (a timeout of `30` where `30000` was meant, outside the schema's range);
  2. **crashes on load**;
  3. **bad only at peak** (healthy for 20 minutes, then fails when traffic crosses a threshold);
  4. **bad for one region's data only**.
  A tick simulation shows the push propagating over a few seconds and servers going red. The readout is **blast radius** in **server-minutes affected**, the same unit for every strategy (AC-0/T3 #18).
- **Scene 2 · Staged.** The same change through stages the learner edits: **canary** (1 server), **one region** (12), **half** (24), **all** (48), each with a **bake time** (slider, minutes) and a **health gate** (an error ratio above the threshold stops the rollout and rolls back). Toggles: *validate before push*, *automatic rollback*.
- **Scene 3 · The schedule.** A `GanttLanes` with `calendarTicks` (K3-12, UTC, stated on screen), one lane per region: stage windows and bake bars, zero-length ticks for *gate passed* (K3-11), and each region's traffic-peak window. A stage that finishes inside a region's peak is a different risk; the plan is a timeline.
- **The way to fail.** With a **5 min** bake and the *bad at peak* change (it shows itself at minute 20), the staged rollout reaches all 48 servers before any gate sees a problem, and the caption says so ("staging only helps if each bake is longer than the change takes to show itself"). With a **25 min** bake the gate stops it at the canary. The learner finds this by moving one slider.
- **Model (pure).** Per stage, exposed servers x (bake + rollback time) gives the closed-form server-minutes for deterministic faults. The simulator ticks once a minute, applies each change's manifest rule, and the gate fails on the first tick where the stage's observed error ratio exceeds the threshold. **Hidden controls (AC-0/T3 #17):** bake and gate exist only in staged mode; in scene 1 they are pinned to constants, and a test sets them to extremes and asserts scene 1's outputs do not change.
**Out of scope.** Flag evaluation and percentage rollouts (`feature-flags`), canary traffic analysis and blue-green (`deployment-strategies`), config-management products, secrets (`secrets-management`).
**Kit.** `CellGrid` (fleet, `rowLabels` = regions), `GanttLanes` + `calendarTicks` + instants (K3-12, K3-11), `Slider` (`format` in minutes), `Toggle`, `Segmented`, `ChipButton`, `Stat`, `Provenance`, `useTickSim` (K3-2).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** healthy server `--ok` tint, failing `--bad`, not yet reached `--dim`, the stage being baked `--hi`, a server rolled back `--info`, a validation refusal `--orange` (rejected before it shipped, not an outage), stage bars `--violet`.
- **Misconception:** "it is only config, so it cannot take us down", and "a staged rollout is safe by definition". A quiz question gives a bake time shorter than the time the fault takes to show and asks whether staging protects.
- **Correctness:**
  - Simulated blast radius equals the **closed form** for deterministic faults (all at once: 48 x detection minutes; staged: stage sizes x bake plus rollback) over a generated set of stage plans.
  - The *bad at peak* cases are pinned (5 min bake reaches 48 servers; 25 min stops at 1).
  - Validation refuses exactly the out-of-range values.
  - Invariant: after a rollback no server runs the new version.
- **Hostile input:** bake and gate sliders clamp; stage sizes are chips from a fixed set, so a plan cannot exceed the fleet.
- **Honesty:** "simulated fleet and traffic, illustrative numbers". "Several publicly reported outages involved a configuration change pushed everywhere at once; read the public postmortems" with no company named.
**Phone.** The 12 x 4 grid at `size="sm"` fits 356 px; the Gantt sits in a `WideRegion minWidth={520}`.

**`gitops` · GitOps & Reconciliation Loops**
Track `ops` · *"Desired state, continuously enforced"* · Builds on: `infrastructure-as-code`
**Tier 2 fence.** `infrastructure-as-code` left this lesson: "Continuous reconciliation from git (T3 `gitops`), module systems and state locking beyond one note." This lesson starts at the loop, not at plan and apply.
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · The loop.** Two panels: **git** (the desired state: a small structured list of three deployments with `image` and `replicas`, and one service) and the **cluster** (live state, drawn as boxes). A **controller** compares them every tick and acts: create, update, delete or nothing. The learner edits git by form (replicas, image-tag chips) and presses **commit**. The controller notices and converges one action per tick, with the diff in a `CodePanel` (`add` / `del` lines).
- **Scene 2 · Drift.** A "manual change" panel does what an engineer in a hurry does: scale a deployment by hand, delete the service, edit an image in the cluster. The next tick reports **drift**. With **auto-sync on** the controller reverts it; off, it only alerts (an out-of-sync badge). A **sync interval** slider (1-60 s) shows the drift window.
- **Scene 3 · The incident trap.** During a simulated load spike the learner scales to 10 by hand. The controller reverts to 3 and the simulated users suffer. The right moves are to **commit the change** or **pause sync as a deliberate, logged override**, not to fight the loop; the override's cost (it can now drift unnoticed) is shown.
- **Scene 4 · Level-triggered.** Toggle *controller restarts and misses events*. An edge-triggered handler that reacts only to "deleted" events misses a deletion that happened while it was down and stays wrong; the level-triggered loop compares full state and converges anyway. A **prune** toggle shows that a resource removed from git is deleted only with pruning on; otherwise it is an orphan.
- **Pull vs push.** A two-box diagram contrasts an in-cluster agent *pulling* from git with a CI job *pushing* with cluster credentials, and states what each changes about where the credentials live. Argo CD and Flux are named as examples only.
**Out of scope.** Real Kubernetes manifests and CRDs, Helm or Kustomize, secrets in git (`secrets-management`), progressive delivery (`deployment-strategies`), the Terraform state model (`infrastructure-as-code`).
**Kit.** `SceneNode` boxes (or `GraphView` fixed layout) for the cluster, `CodePanel` for the diff and the desired state, `Toggle`, `Slider`, `ChipButton` (manual changes), `GanttLanes` (drift windows, optional), `createRecorder`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the desired-state side `--info`; a live resource in sync `--ok`; a drifted resource `--bad`; the controller's current action `--hi`; an orphan `--orange`; a paused sync `--violet`; resources not yet created `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "a controller is a script that runs once on commit" (it is a loop that compares continuously), and "fixing prod by hand is faster, so do it". A quiz question puts a manual hotfix in front of auto-sync and asks what happens next.
- **Correctness:**
  - `reconcile(desired, live, { prune })` returns an action list. Over **500 seeded random (desired, live) pairs**: applying the actions makes `live` equal `desired` on every desired key; a second pass yields no actions (**idempotent**); with prune on no extra keys remain; with prune off extras survive and are reported as orphans.
  - An **independent set-based checker**, not the reconcile code, decides equality.
  - The missed-event case is a named test: the edge-triggered handler ends wrong and the level-triggered one ends right on the same event sequence.
- **Hostile input:** manual changes and commits come from bounded controls (replicas 0-20 clamped, tags from a fixed list); no free YAML is parsed.
- **Honesty:** "a toy controller, not Kubernetes: no API server, no real manifests". That Kubernetes controllers are reconciliation loops is **standard**; link `kubernetes-basics`.
**Phone.** Git and cluster panels stack; the diff `CodePanel` scrolls inside itself (K3-6).

**`query-planning` · Query Planning & EXPLAIN** [domain review]
Track `data` · *"How the database decides"* · Builds on: `db-indexes`
**Tier 2 fences.** `relational-algebra`: "Physical join algorithms belong to T3 `join-algorithms`. Cost-based planning belongs to T3 `query-planning`." `sql-aggregation-windows`: "Query plans and sort/hash aggregation belong to T3 `query-planning`." This lesson owns access paths, cost estimates, statistics and reading a plan; the *join* node's internals are `join-algorithms`, the next lesson.
**Spec.**
- **The table.** `orders`: 100,000 rows on 1,000 pages (a toy 100 rows per page), columns `customer_id` (10,000 distinct), `status` (four values with a skewed distribution: `shipped` 70%, `paid` 20%, `new` 9.5%, `refunded` 0.5%), `created_at`, `total`. The learner picks one of five authored queries (chips, each shown in a `CodePanel` as SQL) and, where the query has a parameter, a value from a bounded control (a customer id 1-10,000 via `parseBoundedInt`, a status chip, a date slider). No free-form SQL is parsed.
- **Scene 1 · SQL becomes a tree.** The chosen query is drawn as a plan tree (`TreeSvg`: Limit, Sort, Seq Scan or Index Scan, Filter), each node with `cost = startup..total`, estimated rows, and (toggle *EXPLAIN ANALYZE*) actual rows. A second panel prints the plan as text in the PostgreSQL style, chipped **shown, not run: from memory of the format**.
- **Scene 2 · Toggle indexes and statistics.** Switches for an index on `customer_id`, on `status`, on `created_at`, and a composite on `(customer_id, created_at)`; and **statistics fresh / stale** (stale = gathered when the table had 1,000 rows with `status` evenly spread). The optimiser re-costs every candidate plan and picks the cheapest; a candidate table (sortable, K3-4) lists all of them with their cost, so the learner sees what lost and by how much.
- **Scene 3 · The crossover.** A `Plot2D` of cost against rows returned: seq scan is a flat line, index scan climbs. A draggable `VLine` sets the selectivity; a slider changes **random page cost**. With the default constants the toy's crossover is **813 rows (0.81% of the table)**; with `random_page_cost` 1.1 (a common setting for fast storage, **common practice, verify**) it moves to **76,447 rows (76.4%)**. The learner sees why "use the index" is not a rule.
- **Scene 4 · Misestimates.** With stale statistics the optimiser believes `status = 'refunded'` returns 25% of the table, picks a seq scan, while the true 0.5% would have been cheaper by an index; the node shows *estimated 25,000, actual 500, off by 50x* (computed). The lesson says what the real remedy is called (refresh statistics, `ANALYZE` in PostgreSQL; the name is from memory).
- **Scene 5 · Sort elimination.** `WHERE customer_id = ? ORDER BY created_at DESC LIMIT 10` has a Sort node; the composite index removes it, and the cost drops. A second query on `status` shows an index that cannot help the ORDER BY.
- **The toy cost model (labelled a simplified model, not PostgreSQL's formulas).** Constants: `seq_page_cost` 1.0, `random_page_cost` 4.0, `cpu_tuple_cost` 0.01, `cpu_index_tuple_cost` 0.005, `cpu_operator_cost` 0.0025 (the PostgreSQL defaults as the author remembers them; **verify against the PostgreSQL documentation, planner cost constants**). Seq scan with one predicate = `P x seq_page_cost + N x cpu_tuple_cost + N x cpu_operator_cost` = 2,250 for these numbers. Index scan with M matching rows = `3 x random_page_cost` (descent) `+ pagesFetched(M) x random_page_cost + M x (cpu_index_tuple_cost + cpu_tuple_cost)`, where `pagesFetched(M) = P x (1 - (1 - 1/P)^M)` (expected distinct pages when M rows land on P pages).
**Out of scope.** Join order and join algorithms (`join-algorithms`), parallel plans, partition pruning, adaptive and plan-caching behaviour, hints, and tuning `work_mem`. Index structure is `db-indexes`.
**Kit.** `TreeSvg` (plan tree, `nodeMark` for the chosen and rejected paths), `CodePanel` (SQL and the text plan; K3-6 keeps long lines inside), `Plot2D` (`Curve`, `VLine onDrag`, `HLine`), `SortTh` / `useSortedRows` (K3-4), `Toggle`, `Segmented`, `Slider` (`format`), `ChipButton`, `Stat` (K3-3), `Provenance` (K3-7), `core/parse` (`parseBoundedInt`).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the chosen plan and the cheapest candidate `--ok`; a rejected candidate `--dim`; the node being costed `--hi`; an estimate that is far from actual `--bad`, with the ratio written; the sort node `--orange` (a costly step, not an error); the crossover line `--violet`; an index in use `--info`.
- **Misconception:** "if an index exists the database will use it", and "the plan is what happened". A quiz question gives a query returning 40% of a table with an index on the filter column and asks what the planner does (and why an estimated plan can differ from what ran).
- **Correctness:**
  - **Against SQLite (`node:sqlite`):** a 2,000-row table generated from the same seeded generator is queried with and without each index. For the queries tagged deterministic (an equality on an indexed column versus no index), the toy's access path agrees with `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN` (`SEARCH ... USING INDEX` vs `SCAN`); the `ORDER BY` query shows a sort step (`USE TEMP B-TREE FOR ORDER BY`) exactly when the toy has a Sort node; the toy's "actual rows" equal `SELECT count(*)` on that data.
  - Cases that are cost-sensitive (the crossover, stale statistics) are **not** compared to SQLite and the lesson says "SQLite decides this differently without statistics".
  - The cost model is checked against hand-computed values (seq 2,250; the 813 and 76,447 crossovers recomputed by an independent search); the cheapest candidate is chosen in every combination of indexes x statistics x query (exhaustive, small).
  - A **broken planner** that ignores `random_page_cost` fails the crossover test.
- **Hostile input:** customer id outside 1-10,000, empty or non-numeric text is refused with a message and the old value kept; the date slider clamps; there is no SQL text field.
- **Honesty:** "toy cost model and a toy table, not PostgreSQL's planner"; the text plan format and constants are **from memory, check the docs**; "SQLite agreement is tested only for the cases marked"; what the planner thinks is an *estimate* until `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` runs it.
**Phone.** The plan tree at 356 px with a `minWidth` from `minWidthForText`; the candidate table scrolls inside a `WideRegion`; controls wrap.

**`join-algorithms` · Join Algorithms: Nested Loop, Hash, Merge**
Track `data` · *"Three ways to match rows"* · Builds on: `query-planning`
**Tier 2 fence.** `relational-algebra`: "Physical join algorithms belong to T3 `join-algorithms`." The join *operator* and outer joins are `sql-basics`; this lesson is how the engine executes it.
**Spec.**
- **The tables.** `customers` (default 8 rows) and `orders` (default 12 rows), each row a `CellGrid` row, joined on `customer_id`. A **size** control (4-16 rows each), a **many-to-many** preset (duplicate keys on both sides), and a **NULL key** preset (rows with a NULL key never match, as in SQL).
- **Scene 1 · Nested loop.** For each outer row the inner table is scanned; every comparison increments a counter, matching rows get a rail (K3-16). A toggle **index on the inner key** turns the inner scan into an index probe that costs a stated number of node visits per probe (the tree height), so the same join does far fewer comparisons.
- **Scene 2 · Hash join.** Build a hash table from the smaller side, probe with the other; buckets are drawn, each build insert and probe lookup counts one operation. A **memory limit** slider (rows that fit) forces the build side to spill: the lesson partitions both sides into `B = ceil(build / memory)` batches, keeps batch 0 in memory, writes the rest out and reads it back, and counts **rows spilled** (a real, computed number).
- **Scene 3 · Merge join.** Both sides sorted on the key (sort cost counted unless the **already sorted** toggle says an index supplies the order), then two pointers advance; duplicate runs show the inner pointer rewinding to the start of the run.
- **Scene 4 · Which one, and when none.** A cost table (one unit defined once: *key comparisons plus hash operations plus sort comparisons*, AC-0/T3 #18) sortable with `SortTh` (K3-4). A **non-equality join** (`a.x < b.y`) is offered: hash and merge are refused with the reason, nested loop is the only option. A **tiny outer, big inner with index** case shows nested loop winning; the large-vs-large equality join shows hash or merge winning.
- All three algorithms produce the same joined rows; a fixed **result panel** shows the output and a check "all three agree" computed live.
**Out of scope.** Join order search, bushy vs left-deep plans, outer and semi joins as operators, parallel and distributed joins, Bloom-filter joins, and costing (`query-planning` set the cost vocabulary).
**Kit.** `CellGrid` with `rowDecor` rails (K3-16), `ArrayStrip` for the merge pointers and sorted sides, `SortTh` / `useSortedRows` (K3-4), `Slider` (size, memory), `Toggle`, `ChipButton` (presets), `Stat` (K3-3), `createRecorder`, `Provenance`, `CodePanel` (SQL).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the outer row being processed `--hi`; the inner row compared `--info`; a match `--ok`; a miss `--dim`; spilled rows `--orange` (a cost, not an error); the hash bucket probed `--violet`; the cheapest algorithm in the table `--ok`, the dearest `--bad`.
- **Misconception:** "hash join is always the fastest", and "a join is a nested loop and nothing else". A quiz question gives a one-row outer table with an index on the inner key and asks which algorithm wins.
- **Correctness:**
  - All three algorithms equal a **brute-force cross product filtered on the key** (as multisets) on 1,000 seeded table pairs, including duplicates on both sides and NULL keys (never matched).
  - Counters match closed forms: nested loop `|R| x |S|`; index nested loop `|R| x height`; hash `|build| + |probe|`; merge pointer advances `<= |R| + |S|` for unique keys.
  - Rows spilled equals an independent count of rows whose hash lands outside batch 0, for both sides.
  - Against SQLite: the three algorithms' output equals SQLite's join on the same seeded data (SQLite's own strategy is a nested loop; the lesson says "SQLite always joins by nested loops, with an automatic index when it helps; verify with `EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN`", and a test checks the plan text).
- **Hostile input:** sizes clamp to 4-16; the memory slider is 2 to the build size; a typed key cell accepts an integer or `NULL` only, anything else refused with a message.
- **Honesty:** "what PostgreSQL's planner would pick is **shown, not run**"; hash-join partitioning is a teaching model of the idea (real engines differ in batching and skew handling, **verify**); counters are in the unit stated once, not seconds.
**Phone.** Two tables stack; the merge strip uses `ArrayStrip cellMax`; the cost table scrolls in a `WideRegion`.

**`mvcc` · MVCC: Multi-Version Concurrency Control** [domain review]
Track `data` · *"Readers never block writers"* · Builds on: `isolation-levels`
**Tier 2 fence.** `optimistic-pessimistic-locking`: "Isolation levels and their anomalies (T3 `isolation-levels`), MVCC internals (T3 `mvcc`) ..." and `isolation-levels` keeps the anomaly list, level names and write skew. This lesson owns **version chains, the visibility rule, long transactions pinning old versions, and cleanup**. It does not re-teach anomalies.
**Spec.**
- **The model (a PostgreSQL-style simplification, stated on screen).** A table of three keys (A, B, C). Each row **version** has `xmin` (the transaction that created it) and `xmax` (the one that deleted or replaced it, or empty). `UPDATE` marks the old version's `xmax` and appends a new version; `DELETE` sets `xmax`. A transaction's **snapshot** records the next transaction id and the set of transactions still active when it was taken. **Visibility:** a version is visible to a transaction iff its creator is that transaction or a transaction that committed before the snapshot (committed, id below the snapshot's next id, not in its active set), **and** its deleter is not such a transaction.
- **Scene 1 · Version chains and snapshots.** Up to three concurrent transactions T1-T3 plus autocommit. Buttons: BEGIN, SELECT, UPDATE (to a value chosen from a chip), DELETE, INSERT, COMMIT, ROLLBACK. The version table is a `CellGrid` with columns key, value, xmin, xmax, and a row rail (K3-16) toned **for the selected transaction**: visible to it `--ok`, created after its snapshot or by an aborted transaction `--dim`, dead to everyone `--bad`. Selecting another transaction recolours the same table, which is the whole point. A **snapshot timing** `Segmented`: *per statement* (each SELECT takes a new snapshot) or *per transaction* (one snapshot at the first statement), so the learner sees two readers get two answers from one table without blocking.
- **Writers vs writers.** UPDATE or DELETE of a row another active transaction has already changed is refused with "T2 would wait for T1 (row locked): finish T1 first". After T1 commits and T2 retries: *per transaction* mode ends with a "could not serialize access due to concurrent update" error and T2 aborts (this is PostgreSQL's REPEATABLE READ behaviour, **verify against its transaction-isolation page**); *per statement* mode updates the newest version.
- **Scene 2 · Vacuum and the long transaction.** A **VACUUM** button removes versions no current or future snapshot can see. The horizon is the oldest snapshot still open. Presets: **idle in transaction** (T1 opens a snapshot and goes quiet; autocommit updates one hot row 50 times; the table grows to 51 versions; VACUUM removes none; commit T1; VACUUM removes 50) and **bloat from updates** (a counter of live vs dead versions and "table size" bar). The lesson names the cost: a single forgotten open transaction stops cleanup everywhere.
- **Scene 3 · Why UPDATE copies.** One row updated twice, drawn as a chain A v1 to v2 to v3 with arrows and the three `xmin/xmax` pairs, plus a note that other engines keep the old versions in an undo log rather than in the table (named, from memory, **verify per engine**).
**Out of scope.** The anomaly list, level names, write skew and serializable certification (`isolation-levels`), locks and deadlocks, index maintenance and HOT updates, transaction-id wraparound (one sentence naming it), distributed MVCC.
**Kit.** `CellGrid` with `rowDecor` (K3-16) and `onCellClick` for picking the row, `Segmented` (transaction, snapshot timing), `ChipButton` (operations, values), `Bar` (table size), `Stat` (K3-3), `Tray` (messages), `createRecorder` for presets, `Provenance` (K3-7), `useShake`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** visible to the selected transaction `--ok`; not visible (too new, or creator aborted) `--dim`; a dead version nobody can see `--bad`; the version being written now `--hi`; the selected transaction's snapshot `--info`; a refused operation `--orange`; the horizon marker `--violet`.
- **Misconception:** "MVCC means readers see the latest data" (they see a snapshot), and "deleting a row frees its space immediately" (the old version stays until cleanup). A quiz question describes an hour-old open transaction and asks why the table keeps growing.
- **Correctness (AC-0/T3 #19 and #20, single-node concurrency):**
  - The visibility function is checked against an **independent serial-replay oracle**: for 500 seeded schedules over three keys and up to four transactions (BEGIN, reads, writes, commit, rollback, VACUUM interleaved), every SELECT in *per transaction* mode returns exactly what you get by applying, in commit order, only the transactions that had committed before its snapshot, plus its own writes.
  - **Safety invariant at every step:** VACUUM never removes a version visible to any active snapshot, so a transaction's repeated SELECT returns the same rows before and after any number of VACUUMs.
  - The removable set equals an independent definition (deleter committed and visible to every active snapshot, or creator aborted).
  - A **broken VACUUM** (horizon = latest committed transaction instead of the oldest snapshot) must violate the invariant on at least one seed in 1 to 200; the test records the lowest such seed and the counterexample trace is shown when the learner turns the broken mode on. A scripted "oops" the model does not produce is a staged lie.
  - The idle-transaction preset's numbers (51 versions, 0 removed, then 50) are pinned.
- **Hostile input:** at most 3 open transactions and 200 versions; past 200 the table refuses new versions with "table full: run VACUUM"; operations that do not apply (SELECT with no transaction begun, COMMIT of nothing) are disabled with a stated reason.
- **Honesty:** `Provenance`: "a simplified PostgreSQL-style model: no hint bits, no frozen ids, no HOT, no wraparound"; the PostgreSQL error text and REPEATABLE READ behaviour are **from memory, check the docs**; other databases (undo-log designs) differ.
**Phone.** The version table is 4 columns by at most 8 rows and fits 356 px at `size="sm"`; transaction pickers stack above it.

**`buffer-pool` · Buffer Pools & Database Page Management**
Track `data` · *"How a DB keeps hot pages in RAM"* · Builds on: `lru-cache`, `memory-hierarchy`
**Out of scope.** The **operating-system page cache**, readahead and `mmap` faults (T3 `mmap-page-cache`, built after this lesson, may link back); the LRU data structure itself (`lru-cache`); write-ahead logging and checkpoints beyond a one-line "dirty pages are flushed later" (`write-ahead-log`).
**Spec.**
- **The pool.** A pool of 8 frames (a `CellGrid`, 2 x 4) over a "disk" of 24 numbered pages. Each frame shows its page, a **pin count**, a **dirty** mark and, for the clock policies, a **usage count**. A request (click a disk page, or run a workload) is a **hit** if the page is in a frame, else a **miss**: a free frame or a victim is chosen by the policy.
- **Policies** (`Segmented`): **LRU**; **Clock** (a sweeping hand that decrements a per-frame usage count, capped at 5 as PostgreSQL's clock sweep is remembered, and evicts the first frame at zero; **verify against the PostgreSQL buffer-manager README**); **Clock + scan ring** (a large sequential scan is confined to a small ring of 4 frames so it cannot flush the rest; PostgreSQL's ring buffers for big scans are named, size from memory, **verify**).
- **Scene 1 · A hot set.** A seeded workload (Zipf-like over the 24 pages, skew slider) with a live **hit ratio** (`MetricMeter`, K3-13) and counters: hits, misses, disk reads.
- **Scene 2 · The scan pollutes the pool.** Press **run a full scan** (pages 0-23 in order). Under LRU and Clock the hot pages are evicted and the hit ratio collapses; under the scan ring it barely moves. The before and after hit ratios are computed, not drawn.
- **Scene 3 · Pins and dirty pages.** Click a frame to **pin** it (a running query is using the page): eviction skips pinned frames. Pin all eight and request a missing page: "no unpinned frame: the request waits or fails" (real systems differ; PostgreSQL reports an error when no unpinned buffer exists, **verify**). Write requests mark pages dirty; evicting a dirty page costs a **write-back** counted separately; a **checkpoint** button flushes all dirty pages.
- **Scene 4 · Size it.** A pool-size slider (4-16 frames) plots hit ratio against size for the current workload (a `Plot2D` curve computed by running the workload at each size), showing the working-set knee.
**Kit.** `CellGrid` with `rowDecor` (K3-16) for frame state, `MetricMeter` (K3-13), `Plot2D`, `Segmented`, `Slider` (`format`), `Toggle`, `ChipButton`, `Stat` (K3-3), `core/random` (`mulberry32`), `useSimulation` or `createTickSim` (K3-2), `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the frame holding the requested page `--hi`; a hit `--ok`; a miss `--bad`; a pinned frame `--violet` ring; a dirty page `--orange` mark; the clock hand `--info`; empty frames `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "a bigger or smarter cache always helps", and "LRU is scan-proof". A quiz question gives a hot table plus a nightly full scan and asks what the morning's first queries see.
- **Correctness:**
  - The LRU policy equals an **independent reference LRU** (a plain array-based implementation) on seeded Zipf traces, hit for hit; Clock equals a hand-worked sequence in the data test.
  - Invariants at every step: a pinned page is never evicted; frames in use never exceed capacity; `hits + misses` equals requests; write-backs equal dirty evictions plus checkpoint flushes.
  - The scan-pollution claim is a test: the hot-set hit ratio measured after a scan under LRU is lower than under the scan ring on the same seed, by a stated margin (computed, then pinned).
  - A **broken policy** that evicts pinned frames fails the first invariant.
- **Hostile input:** page requests are buttons on a fixed disk; sliders clamp; no typed numbers.
- **Honesty:** "a teaching model: real pools hash pages to buffers, use latches, and run a background writer". Numbers such as the usage-count cap and ring size are **from memory, check the source**; the workload is **simulated, seeded** (seed shown).
**Phone.** The frames grid fits 356 px at `size="md"`; the disk strip is two rows of 12; the size plot is one column.

**`turing-machines` · Turing Machines**
Track `lang` · *"A tape, a head, and all of computation"* · Builds on: none (registered context: `dfa-nfa`)
**Spec.**
- **The machine.** A one-tape deterministic machine: a finite set of states, the tape alphabet (blank is `_`), and rules `state symbol -> write move next` where `move` is `L`, `R` or `S` (stay; a convenience that adds no power, one key idea says so). The tape is unbounded in both directions (a sparse map; moving left of the first cell extends it with blanks). A machine **halts** when it reaches a state named `halt`, `accept` or `reject`, and is **stuck** when no rule matches a non-halting state (reported as "stuck in q1 reading 1", never as success). It is **budget-exhausted** after 5,000 steps.
- **Scene 1 · Program binary increment (the task).** A `TapeStrip` (K3-17) shows 17 cells around the head and scrolls with it, over a `TextArea` holding the transition table. The learner edits the table (`parseTmTable`, refusals shown inline: unknown move, missing arrow, a duplicate `(state, symbol)` rule "q0 1 already has a rule on line 2", a name over 8 characters, more than 40 rules, a line over 40 characters). Input is a binary string up to 16 characters; **Run**, **Step**, **Back** and a speed control. A **Check** button runs the table against all binary strings of length 0-4 plus 12 seeded longer ones, compares each result tape with the oracle (`value + 1`, in `BigInt`), and shows the first failing input with the tape it left and the one expected. A *reveal* chip loads the author's table after a first attempt.

**`halting-problem` · The Halting Problem & Undecidability** [domain review]
Track `lang` · *"Some questions no program can ever answer"* · Builds on: `turing-machines`
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · The diagonal table (the proof, manipulated).** A 6 x 6 `CellGrid`: row i is "program P_i" and column j is "input j"; each cell holds **H** (halts) or **L** (loops), and it is **what a hypothetical decider claims**. The learner fills it in by clicking cells (or *randomise*, seeded), any way they like. **Build D** appends the row `D(j)` = the opposite of the diagonal cell `(j, j)`. The lesson then scans every row and rings the column where it differs from D: **every row differs from D at its own diagonal cell, whatever the learner entered.** The learner tries to find a row equal to D and cannot. **D is a program too** (it is row 7): add the 7th row and the new diagonal cell `(D, D)` must be the opposite of itself; whichever of H or L the learner clicks is contradicted, shown in `--bad`. That is the proof, in the learner's hands.
- **Scene 2 · Defeat your own predictor.** A library of 8 real machines run by K3-17 (increment on `1011`: halts in 8 steps; a two-state flip loop (repeats a configuration: **certified loop**); a machine that walks right forever writing 1s (loop, no repeating configuration: certified by the lesson's recorded argument); and the busy-beaver champions for 2, 3 and 4 states, which halt after **6, 21 and 107 steps** leaving 4, 5 and 13 ones; the author ran them: tables `1RB 1LB 1LA 1RH`, `1RB 1RH 1LB 0RC 1LC 1LA`, `1RB 1LB 1LA 0LC 1RH 1LD 1RD 0RA` in the usual state-by-symbol notation, **verify against the busy-beaver literature**). The learner builds a **predictor** from a menu (no code is typed): *budget* (run N steps, say HALTS if it stopped else LOOPS; N is a slider), *cycle* (stop and say LOOPS on a repeated configuration, HALTS on a halt, guess LOOPS after 1,000 steps), *always LOOPS*, each optionally allowed to answer **UNKNOWN**. A scoreboard compares each verdict with the ground truth. Budget 50 calls the 107-step machine a loop: wrong; raise N to 200 and it is right.
- **Then D is built from the learner's predictor.** `D(M)` asks the predictor about `(M, M)`, then does the opposite: loop if told HALTS (a configuration repeats: certified), halt at once if told LOOPS. The lesson **runs D on its own description**: the predictor's verdict is shown, D's real behaviour is executed and certified, and they disagree, for every predictor that always answers. A predictor allowed to say **UNKNOWN** is not contradicted by D, and the scoreboard says why it is no decider: it did not answer on k of 9 inputs.
- **Scene 3 · Rice's theorem, sorted.** Eight properties as cards, sorted into *decidable by reading the code*, *decidable by running a bounded number of steps*, *undecidable (Rice)*, *trivial*. Examples: "has more than 10 lines" and "contains the word while" (syntactic), "halts within 1,000 steps on input 0" (bounded: run it), "halts on input 0", "prints 7 on some input", "is the constant-zero function", "two programs compute the same function" (non-trivial semantic: undecidable), "computes some function" (trivial: every program does). A wrong sort explains the property's class. Rice's theorem is stated in full: every non-trivial property of the function a program computes is undecidable.
- **What it does not say.** A closing panel: it does not forbid analysing *many* programs; static analysers and linters answer "yes", "no" or "maybe" (sound but incomplete), and a bounded check is always decidable.
**Out of scope.** The reduction technique in general, oracle machines and Turing degrees, Gödel's incompleteness theorems, busy-beaver theory beyond the three known champions, Kolmogorov complexity.
**Kit.** K3-17 (`runTm`, `Tm`, `TapeStrip` for the 107-step run, optional), `CellGrid` (`onCellClick`, `highlight`), `Segmented` (predictor), `Slider`, `ChipButton`, `Tray` (card sort), `Stat`, `createRecorder`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the diagonal cells `--hi`; H `--ok`, L `--orange`; D's row `--violet`; the cell where a row differs from D `--bad` ring; a certified loop `--bad`; **did not halt within N steps** `--dim` (unknown, not proved); a correct predictor verdict `--ok`, wrong `--bad`; UNKNOWN `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "the halting problem means we cannot tell whether any program halts", and "a program that ran for a long time is looping". A quiz question gives a program that stopped after 107 steps under a 50-step budget and asks what the budget run proved.
- **Correctness:**
  - **Exhaustive:** for all 2^9 tables of 3 x 3 and all 2^16 tables of 4 x 4, the diagonal-flip row differs from every existing row.
  - For every always-answering predictor in the menu, D(D) behaves opposite to the predictor's verdict, by actually running D (halts in 1 step or repeats a configuration, certified).
  - `runTm` on the library equals an **independent simulator** on the 8 machines; the three busy-beaver machines give exactly 6, 21 and 107 steps with 4, 5 and 13 ones.
  - A **caption test** walks every state of the lesson and fails if "never halts" or "runs forever" appears without a certificate in the data.

"Busy-beaver values are known results, here checked by running them (**verify against the literature**)". The lesson says what is **shown** (the diagonal construction, exhaustive for small tables) versus what is **argued** (the general theorem).

**`lambda-calculus` · Lambda Calculus** [domain review]
Track `lang` · *"All of computation from just functions"* · Builds on: `functional-programming`
**Spec.**
- **The language.** Terms are variables, abstractions `λx.M` and applications `M N`. Surface syntax accepts `\x.M` or `λx.M`, juxtaposition for application (left-associative; an abstraction extends as far right as possible), parentheses, single-letter or letter-digit identifiers, and a number `3` as sugar for the Church numeral `λf.λx.f (f (f x))`. Named chips insert `true`, `false`, `if`, `and`, `succ`, `add`, `mul`, `Ω` and `Y` as terms (shown expanded).
- **Scene 1 · The stepper.** A term typed or chosen from presets is shown as a tree (`TreeSvg`: `λx` nodes, `@` application nodes, variable leaves, each bound variable toned like its binder) and as text with every **redex** `(λx.M) N` highlighted. The learner **clicks a redex** to reduce it, or uses **normal order** / **applicative order** step and **run to normal form** (a `Segmented` strategy). The reduction shows the substitution happening: each free occurrence of `x` in `M` is ringed, then replaced by `N`.
- **Scene 2 · Capture.** `(λx.λy.x) y` is offered. The *naive* substitution gives `λy.y` (the free `y` was captured: wrong, now the identity); the lesson **refuses** that step, says why ("`y` is free in the argument but the body binds a `y`"), and performs the **α-rename** of the inner binder first, so the correct result is `(λx.λy.x) y → λy1.y` (the free `y` stays free). A toggle *naive substitution* is available as a **labelled wrong mode** so the learner sees the wrong result and the lesson flags it.
- **Scene 3 · Build it.** Task cards: `succ`, `add`, `mul`, `and`, `not`. The learner types a term `T`; **Check** applies `T` to Church numerals (all pairs 0-4 for the arithmetic tasks, or both booleans) and compares the normal form with the expected one **up to α-equivalence**. A hint reveals the shape (`add = λm.λn.λf.λx. m f (n f x)`) after a first try. A wrong term shows the first input it fails and the normal form it produced.
- **Scene 4 · Strategy matters.** `(λx.λy.y) Ω` reaches `λy.y` in normal order and **does not reach a normal form within 200 steps** in applicative order; `Ω = (λx.x x)(λx.x x)` reduces to itself after one step (a **certified** loop: the term is identical); `Y f` grows without reaching a normal form (budget message, no "never"). A *size guard* stops a term that grows past 2,000 nodes.
- **Scene 5 · One notation, many programs.** Church booleans and numerals side by side with the JavaScript closures of the same idea, as an analogy only (JavaScript evaluates arguments first, so `Ω`-style code hangs there; the lesson says so in words and runs nothing).
**Out of scope.** Types and the simply typed calculus (`type-systems` names them), η-reduction and combinators beyond `Y` (named), the Church-Rosser theorem's proof (the lesson shows results agreeing on a corpus and states the theorem), de Bruijn indices (used internally only; a note names them).
**Kit.** `TreeSvg` (term tree, per-node tone), `CodePanel` (term text with `renderLine` redex highlighting; K3-6), `TextField` + a **hand-written parser** through `core/parse` refusals, `Segmented` (strategy), `ChipButton` (named terms, presets), `Slider` (step budget, 10-200), `createRecorder`, `Stat`, `Provenance`. The reducer is local (`model.ts`, one user).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the redex about to reduce `--hi`; the argument being substituted `--info`; the occurrences replaced `--ok`; a captured variable and a refused step `--bad`; the α-renamed variable `--orange`; a bound variable shares its binder's categorical tone, a free variable is `muted`; a step budget stop `--dim` ("did not reach a normal form within N steps").
- **Misconception:** "substitution is just find and replace", and "every term reduces to a value". A quiz question applies `λx.λy.x` to a free `y` and asks what the result is, and another asks whether a term that has no normal form under one strategy has none under another.
- **Correctness:**
  - **Independent oracle:** a second reducer working on **de Bruijn indices** (shifting instead of renaming) reduces the same 1,000 seeded closed terms (depth up to 5, budget 100, normal order) and its normal forms equal the lesson's up to α-equivalence, decided by converting both to de Bruijn.
  - Church arithmetic: `add`, `mul`, `succ` on 0-8 equal JavaScript `+`, `*`, `+ 1` after decoding; the booleans' truth tables match.
  - Where normal order and applicative order both terminate, the normal forms are α-equal (a corpus test); `(λx.λy.y) Ω` terminates in one and not the other (pinned); `Ω` steps to itself.
  - The **naive substitution** mode is tested to give a different (wrong) result on the capture example and the lesson's checker is tested to reject it.
  - Printing then parsing round-trips for every generated term.
- **Hostile input:** input capped at 160 characters, nesting at 40, 300 nodes; unbalanced parentheses, an unknown character or an empty term are refused with a message and the old term kept; the step budget and the 2,000-node guard stop everything else; **no `eval` and no `new Function`** on learner text, only the hand-written parser and reducer (CLAUDE.md).
- **Honesty:** "the untyped calculus with Church encodings; the JavaScript comparison is an analogy". Which reduction strategy a real language uses is stated for JavaScript only and hedged.
**Phone.** The tree sits in a `WideRegion` with a `minWidth` from `minWidthForText`; text and controls stack; the build task is a single column.

**`shortest-paths-advanced` · Bellman-Ford & Floyd-Warshall**
Track `dsa` · *"Negative edges and all-pairs"* · Builds on: `graph-search`
**Spec.**
- **Graph editor (all scenes).** An editable weighted directed `GraphView` of at most 7 nodes, integer weights from -9 to 9, edited by click (select an edge) and ± buttons (and the keyboard path `GraphView` already has). Presets: **textbook** (five nodes `s t y x z`, edges `s->t 6, s->y 7, t->x 5, t->y 8, t->z -4, y->x -3, y->z 9, x->t -2, z->s 2, z->x 7`; distances from `s` are `t 2, y 7, x 4, z -2`); **negative cycle** (the same graph with `x->t` changed to -6, so `t->x->t` sums to -1); **Dijkstra breaks** (`s->a 2, s->b 3, b->a -2`); **arbitrage** (currencies as nodes, edge weight `-ln(rate)` for invented rates USD to EUR 0.90, EUR to GBP 0.90, GBP to USD 1.30; the cycle sums to about -0.0516, so converting round the cycle multiplies money by about 1.053).
- **Scene 1 · Bellman-Ford in rounds.** Each round relaxes every edge once in a stated order; the distance table (`ArrayStrip`) updates, a relaxed edge is marked, and a round counter shows how many rounds changed anything. After round k every shortest path of at most k edges is final (a caption computed from the data). The textbook preset converges in round 2 and the algorithm **stops early** after round 3 (no change; with the listed edge order, author-run: 6, 1, 0 changes in rounds 1 to 3). A **round n** check: if any edge still relaxes, a **negative cycle** is reachable and the lesson flashes the cycle and lists the nodes that have **no shortest path** (those reachable from it, found by a search from the still-relaxing vertices); their distance reads "no shortest path", not a big negative number.
- **Scene 2 · Why not Dijkstra.** The *Dijkstra breaks* preset runs Dijkstra with a visited set beside Bellman-Ford: Dijkstra finalises `a` at 2 before it sees `b->a`, and reports 2; the true distance is 1. A note says a Dijkstra variant that re-inserts nodes can recover in this tiny graph but loses its guarantee (named, not shown).
- **Scene 3 · Floyd-Warshall, one k at a time.** A `CellGrid` distance matrix. Stepping `k` allows node `k` as an intermediate: cell `(i, j)` becomes `min(d[i][j], d[i][k] + d[k][j])`; changed cells are marked and the cells read are ringed. A path-recovery control walks the `next` matrix to print one shortest path. A negative value on the **diagonal** is the negative-cycle signal; the lesson stops at the first such `k` and says so (entries can grow very fast once a negative cycle exists, so the lesson does not keep going).
- **Scene 4 · Cost.** Rounds x edges against `k` x n x n as computed operation counts for the graph on screen (O(VE) and O(V^3), stated with the counts, one unit: relaxations).
**Out of scope.** Dijkstra and A* (`graph-search`), Johnson's algorithm and potentials (named), SPFA and other queue-based variants (named), shortest paths in DAGs (`topological-sort` names it), real exchange markets (the rates are invented, the lesson says arbitrage is the idea, not advice).
**Kit.** `GraphView` (editable, `edgeMark`, `nodeMark`), `ArrayStrip` (distances), `CellGrid` (FW matrix, `highlight`), `ChipButton` (presets), `Segmented` (algorithm), `createRecorder`, `Stat` (K3-3), `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the edge being relaxed `--hi`; an edge that lowered a distance `--ok`; a negative edge drawn `--info` with its sign shown; a negative cycle `--bad`; a node with no shortest path `--bad` outline and "no shortest path"; a settled distance `--ok`, unreached `--dim`; the intermediate `k` `--violet`.
- **Misconception:** "a negative edge is the same as a negative cycle", and "Dijkstra works with negative edges if you are careful". A quiz question uses the *Dijkstra breaks* graph and asks for the distance to `a`.
- **Correctness:**
  - Bellman-Ford distances equal a **brute-force enumeration of all simple paths** (graphs of up to 6 nodes, seeded, 1,000 graphs) when there is no negative cycle; Floyd-Warshall from every source equals Bellman-Ford.
  - Negative-cycle detection equals a **brute-force enumeration of simple cycles**' sums on the same graphs; the set of nodes with no shortest path equals reachability from any negative cycle's nodes.
  - The textbook preset gives `t 2, y 7, x 4, z -2`; the negative-cycle preset is flagged and the arbitrage cycle sum is -0.0516 within 1e-4 (computed from the rates).
  - The Dijkstra-with-visited-set variant is **tested to be wrong** on the third preset (reports 2, truth 1); the round-count claim in each caption is computed from the run.
- **Hostile input:** weights clamp to -9..9; at most 7 nodes; a self-loop or a duplicate edge is refused with a message; the editor never accepts a typed number outside the range.
- **Honesty:** "the arbitrage graph uses invented rates"; edge-processing order is stated because round counts depend on it.

**Kit.** `GraphView` with `underlay` (hulls, K3-5), `nodeColor`, `nodeMark`, `edgeMark`, editable; `ArrayStrip` (finish stack), `ChipButton` (presets, start order), `Segmented` (pass), `createRecorder`, `Stat`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the DFS current node `--hi`; a finished node `--ok` tick; a node on the stack `--info`; each component a categorical colour (never `--bad`/`--ok` as decoration) with its hull in the same tone; reversed edges `--violet` dashed during pass 2; a cycle-closing edge you just added `--orange`.

**`network-flow` · Max Flow & Min Cut**
Track `dsa` · *"Push as much as you can through the pipes"* · Builds on: none (context: `graph-search`)
**Spec.**
- **Network editor.** A directed capacity graph of at most 8 nodes with a source `s` and sink `t`, integer capacities 1-99. Presets: **textbook** (`s, v1, v2, v3, v4, t`; `s->v1 16, s->v2 13, v1->v3 12, v2->v1 4, v2->v4 14, v3->v2 9, v3->t 20, v4->v3 7, v4->t 4`; max flow **23**); **the unlucky one** (`s->a 1000, s->b 1000, a->b 1, a->t 1000, b->t 1000`); **pipeline** (a chain with one narrow pipe).
- **Scene 1 · Augment.** The learner clicks a path from `s` to `t` in the **residual graph** (or presses *shortest path* for Edmonds-Karp); the bottleneck is pushed, edges show `flow / capacity`, and a **residual view** toggle redraws the graph as the residual network (forward spare capacity, plus a backward edge for every unit of flow that could be undone). A path may use a backward edge, which is how a bad earlier choice is repaired. The flow value and the augmentation count update.
- **Scene 2 · The unlucky order.** On the unlucky preset a scripted option, **adversarial order** (labelled *scripted*), alternates `s-a-b-t` and `s-b-a-t` through the cross edge, adding 1 each time: **2,000 augmentations** to reach flow 2,000. **Shortest path first** (Edmonds-Karp) needs **2**. The counts are computed from the run, not typed. The lesson says a naive path choice can be slow, and that the bound for shortest-path-first does not depend on the capacities.
- **Scene 3 · Drag the cut.** The learner clicks nodes to move them into the **S side** (`s` always in, `t` always out); the cut's **capacity** is the sum of capacities of edges going from S to T only (edges back from T to S do not count, a stated rule and a common slip), updating live next to the current flow. Any cut is at least the flow. At the end the lesson shows the **min cut** (the nodes still reachable from `s` in the final residual graph, as an underlay region, K3-5) with its saturated edges in `--bad`, and its capacity equals the max flow: 23 on the textbook preset (`v1->v3 12 + v4->v3 7 + v4->t 4`).
- **Scene 4 · What it models.** Three one-line reductions named, one shown: bipartite matching as a unit-capacity network (the next lesson), capacity planning and bottleneck finding, project selection (named).
**Out of scope.** Dinic and push-relabel (named), real-valued capacities (Ford-Fulkerson with irrational capacities may not terminate, a stated aside), min-cost flow, multi-commodity flow, circulations with lower bounds.
**Kit.** `GraphView` with `underlay` (S-side region, K3-5), `edgeMark` (flow, saturation), editable; `Toggle` (residual view), `Segmented` (path choice), `ChipButton` (presets, adversarial order), `Stat` (K3-3: flow, cut capacity, augmentations), `createRecorder`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the augmenting path `--hi`; flow on an edge `--info` with a proportional fill; a saturated edge `--bad`; a backward residual edge `--violet` dashed; the S side region `--ok` tint, the T side `--dim`; the cut's crossing edges `--bad`; spare capacity `muted`.
- **Misconception:** "once you push flow along a path you cannot take it back", and "the min cut counts every edge between the two sides". A quiz question gives a graph where greedy path choice gets stuck at 1 below the max and asks what repairs it.
- **Correctness:**
  - Max flow from Edmonds-Karp equals the **minimum over all 2^(n-2) cuts** (brute-force enumeration, up to 8 nodes) on 1,000 seeded random networks (the max-flow min-cut theorem verified, not assumed).
  - Invariants on every result flow: capacity respected on every edge, conservation at every node except `s` and `t`, flow value equals net flow out of `s`.
  - The textbook preset gives 23 and the cut above; **the unlucky preset gives 2,000 augmentations in adversarial order and 2 in shortest-path order** (pinned); the augmentation count for the textbook preset is pinned for the builder's BFS order (3 with ascending node order, author-run).
  - Cut capacity counts only S-to-T edges (a test with a back edge); a flow with a conservation violation is rejected by the checker (a **broken variant** test).
- **Hostile input:** at most 8 nodes, capacities clamp to 1-99, `s` and `t` are fixed; self-loops and parallel edges are refused with a message.
- **Honesty:** the adversarial order is **scripted** (chipped), chosen to show the worst case; a real DFS may or may not hit it.
**Phone.** At most 8 nodes in 356 px; flow labels sit in the graph with the edge-label halo (K3-5); the residual view is a toggle, not a second graph.

**`bipartite-matching` · Bipartite Matching**
Track `dsa` · *"Pair everyone up, optimally"* · Builds on: `network-flow`
**Spec.**
- **Editor.** Two columns, workers on the left and jobs on the right (at most 6 each), edges toggled by clicking a worker and then a job (and the keyboard path). Presets: **greedy fails** (`W1: J1, J2; W2: J1; W3: J3; W4: J2, J4`); **no perfect matching** (`W1, W2, W3` all want only `J1, J2`); **balanced 5 x 5**.
- **Scene 1 · Greedy vs augmenting.** *Greedy* takes the first free job for each worker in order and gets stuck: on the first preset it matches 3 of 4 (W1-J1, W3-J3, W4-J2) and W2 is left. Pressing **augment from W2** finds the **alternating path** W2-J1-W1-J2-W4-J4 (unmatched, matched, unmatched, matched, unmatched edges, ending at a free job), flips it, and the matching grows to 4. The learner can also click an unmatched worker to search for a path themselves; when none exists the lesson says so.
- **Scene 2 · When it cannot be done.** On *no perfect matching*, the search from the stuck worker explores every reachable alternating path and fails; the workers it reached form a **Hall violator**: `{W1, W2, W3}` has only `{J1, J2}` as neighbours, and 3 workers cannot fit 2 jobs. Hall's condition is stated (a matching that covers all workers exists iff every set of workers has at least as many neighbours as members) and the violator is highlighted. A toggle shows the **minimum vertex cover** of the same size as the maximum matching (König's theorem, bipartite graphs only).
- **Scene 3 · As a flow.** A toggle redraws the matching as a unit-capacity network with `s` and `t` added, so the alternating path is drawn as the augmenting path of `network-flow`, with the flow value equal to the matching size. This scene **redraws the Kuhn matching on the unit network**; the lesson runs no second flow algorithm and shares no code with `network-flow` (a lesson never imports another lesson's files, and batches do not edit `src/core`). The flow value is checked against an independent Edmonds-Karp **in the test only**; if a third lesson needs max flow, the report files a Phase 2 request for `core/flow.ts`.
- **Scene 4 · Costs (optional view).** A 4 x 4 cost matrix (default `[[9,2,7,8],[6,4,3,7],[5,8,1,8],[7,6,9,4]]`): **greedy per row** costs 14, the **optimal assignment** costs 13 (W1-J2, W2-J1, W3-J3, W4-J4), found by an O(n^3) implementation of the Hungarian method and verified by brute force. The method's steps are **not animated** (the lesson says so and names it); the result and the greedy gap are computed.
**Out of scope.** Hopcroft-Karp (named), stable matching and the Gale-Shapley algorithm (named), matching in general (non-bipartite) graphs, Hungarian-method internals beyond the result.
**Kit.** `GraphView` (fixed two-column positions, `edgeMark`, clicking edges), `ChipButton` (presets), `Toggle` (flow view, vertex cover), `CellGrid` (cost matrix, `highlight` for the chosen assignment), `Stat`, `createRecorder`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the current alternating path `--hi`; matched edges `--ok`; unmatched candidate edges `muted`; an edge flipped by the augmentation `--info` ring; the Hall violator set `--bad` outline with its neighbours `--orange`; unmatched workers `--dim`; the vertex cover nodes `--violet`.
- **Misconception:** "a maximal matching is a maximum matching" (greedy gets stuck), and "if my first choices clash there is no solution". A quiz question gives the greedy-fails preset and asks whether a 4-matching exists.
- **Correctness:**
  - Matching size equals a **brute-force maximum over all edge subsets** (up to 6 x 6, seeded, 2,000 graphs) and equals the max flow of the unit network from an independent Edmonds-Karp in the test.
  - When no perfect matching exists the Hall violator is checked by brute force: its neighbourhood is strictly smaller than the set; König's cover has exactly the matching's size and touches every edge.
  - The cost scene: Hungarian cost equals the **minimum over all n! permutations** (n up to 6, seeded) and the default matrix gives greedy 14 and optimal 13 (pinned).
  - The augmenting path on the first preset is the path above, pinned.
- **Hostile input:** at most 6 workers and 6 jobs; edge toggles only between columns; cost entries clamp to 1-99.
- **Honesty:** the Hungarian method is **computed, not animated**, stated on screen.
**Phone.** Two columns at 356 px with 6 nodes each; the cost matrix fits at `size="sm"`.

**`graph-coloring` · Graph Coloring & Scheduling**

**Spec.**

Presets: **crown graph** (8 nodes `a1..a4`, `b1..b4`, `ai` joined to `bj` for every `j != i`; it is 2-colourable), **exam timetable** (courses as nodes, an edge when a student takes both, so the colour is the time slot), **register interference** (variables that are live together), **odd cycle** (5-cycle needs 3 colours), **complete graph K5**.

**`entropy-information` · Entropy & Information Theory** [domain review]
Track `math` · *"How surprised should you be?"* · Builds on: none (context: `logs-exponentials`, `huffman-coding`)
**Tier 2 fences.** `error-detection-correction`: "entropy and coding bounds stay in T3 `entropy-information`." `huffman-coding` already states bits per symbol land in `[entropy, entropy + 1)`; this lesson is where entropy itself is defined, so it links `huffman-coding` for the code and does not rebuild the tree scene.
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · The biased coin.** A probability slider `p` (heads) and a `Plot2D` of `H(p) = -p log2 p - (1-p) log2 (1-p)` with a draggable point. Readouts: the **surprise** of heads (`-log2 p`) and of tails, and entropy as their average weighted by probability. Pinned values: `H(0.5) = 1`, `H(0.25) = 0.8113`, `H(0.1) = 0.4690`, `H(0.01) = 0.0808`, and `H(0) = H(1) = 0` (the convention `0 log 0 = 0` is stated). A *block coding* panel codes `k` flips at a time with a Huffman code over the 2^k outcomes at `p = 0.1` and shows bits per flip falling toward the entropy: **1.0000, 0.6450, 0.5327, 0.4926** for `k = 1..4` against 0.4690 (author-run; the test recomputes them).
- **Scene 2 · Entropy of typed text.** The learner types or picks text (at most 500 characters, counted by code point). The lesson counts character frequencies and shows `H` in bits per character and `n x H` in bits, beside 8 bits per character for plain ASCII. The caption is exact: this is the **order-0 entropy** (it treats characters as independent draws); a better model (words, repeats) can beat it, which is `lz-compression`'s whole point. `aaaaaaaa` gives 0, `abababab` gives 1 bit per character, `mississippi` gives 1.823 and `password` gives 2.75 bits per character, 22 bits in all.
- **Scene 3 · Is it a password strength?** A deliberate warning scene. The order-0 figure of `password` is **not** its strength; strength comes from how the password was **generated**: `L` symbols drawn uniformly from an alphabet of `N` give `L x log2 N` bits (12 characters from 62 letters and digits: 71.45 bits; four words from a 7,776-word list: 51.70 bits), and a human-chosen string is guessed by dictionary, not by uniform search. The learner sets `L` and `N` or picks a generator and sees the bits; the lesson names the difference between entropy of a *distribution* and a number computed from one sample. (Links the later `randomness-csprng` by name only.)
- **Scene 4 · Twenty questions.** A seeded distribution over 8 items (`0.35, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.08, 0.06, 0.04, 0.02`; entropy **2.571 bits**). The learner builds a yes/no question tree by splitting the set at each node (chips) and sees the **expected number of questions** `sum p x depth` live. Splitting evenly by count (3 questions each) gives 3.000; the optimal tree (the same tree Huffman's merge builds) gives **2.63**, never below the entropy. A **hint** button splits by probability mass.
- **Scene 5 · The cost of a wrong belief.** A 3-outcome true distribution (default `0.5, 0.25, 0.25`) and a believed `q` the learner drags (three linked sliders that renormalise). **Cross-entropy** `H(p, q)` and the extra bits `H(p, q) - H(p)` (the KL divergence) update; it is zero only when `q = p`, and `q` uniform costs **0.085 bits** per outcome here. Named link: this is the log loss of `classification-metrics`.
**Out of scope.** Channel capacity, mutual information (named), Kolmogorov complexity (named), arithmetic coding, the formal source coding theorem's proof, the Huffman tree itself (`huffman-coding`), password policies (`security-principles`).
**Kit.** `Plot2D` (`Curve`, `Points onDrag`, `Bars` with per-bar tone from K3-1), `Slider` with `format`, `TextField` / `TextArea` with `core/parse` limits, `core/huffman` (the **new kit item**), `ChipButton`, `Stat` (K3-3 multi-line sub), `createRecorder`, `Provenance`, `core/random` (`mulberry32`).
**AC.**
- **Watch:** probability mass on a bar `--info`; the current symbol's surprise `--hi`; bits above the entropy floor `--bad` (the costly side); the optimal tree's expected depth `--ok`; the entropy floor as an `HLine` `--violet`; a learner tree that sits above optimal `--orange` with the gap written; unreachable options `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "entropy measures how random the text looks, so it is a password strength", and "a smart enough code can beat the entropy". A quiz question gives `password`'s 22 bits and asks whether it takes 2^22 guesses to crack.
- **Correctness:**
  - `H` equals the pinned values and the closed form; empirical entropy from counts equals the independent form `log2 n - (1/n) sum c log2 c` on 1,000 seeded strings.
  - **Optimum oracle:** the minimum expected questions over all binary question trees, computed by an independent dynamic programme over subsets (`opt(S) = w(S) + min over splits of opt(A) + opt(S \ A)`, up to 10 items), equals the Huffman average on seeded distributions, and every learner tree is `>= H` (a property test over random trees, the Gibbs and Kraft bound).
  - Block-coding bits per flip equal the pinned four values; `H(p, q) >= H(p)` with equality only at `q = p` on 1,000 seeded pairs; `L x log2 N` values equal the pinned ones; password `password` gives 2.75 per character.
  - A **broken Huffman** (merging the two heaviest) is rejected: it exceeds the optimum on a seeded distribution.
- **Hostile input:** text is capped at 500 code points; empty text gives "no symbols", not NaN; `L` is 1-64 and `N` is 2-256 through bounded fields; the believed `q` cannot contain a zero where `p` is positive without the lesson saying cross-entropy is infinite there (shown as "unbounded", not a huge number).
- **Honesty:** "entropy here is order-0 on one sample, an estimate, not the text's true information"; `Provenance`: "seeded toy distribution"; the diceware list size 7,776 is stated as a **standard figure, from memory, verify** (it is `6^5`).

**`lz-compression` · Dictionary Compression (LZ77 & DEFLATE)**
Track `math` · *"Say it once, point back to it forever"* · Builds on: `huffman-coding`, `entropy-information`
**Tier 2 fence.** `huffman-coding`: "The real DEFLATE format (canonical codes plus LZ77) belongs to T3 `lz-compression`." This lesson therefore owns **canonical codes** and the real **fixed-Huffman DEFLATE block**.
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · LZ77 tokens.** Typed or preset text (at most 200 characters): `abracadabra abracadabra`, `aaaaaaaaaa`, a repeated JSON-like line, random letters. A **window** slider (4-64 characters; DEFLATE's is 32 KiB, stated) and a **minimum match** selector (2-4, default 3 as in DEFLATE). A greedy parser walks the text: at each position it takes the **longest match** inside the window (ties go to the nearest), emits `(distance, length)` for a match of at least the minimum, else a literal. Arrows point back from each match; the token stream builds below. `abracadabra abracadabra` gives `a b r a c a d (7,4) ' ' (12,11)`: 10 tokens for 23 characters (author-run).
- **Scene 2 · Decode, including overlap.** The learner steps the decoder, which **copies one character at a time**: a match with `length > distance` (`aaaaaaaaaa` is `a` then `(1, 9)`) reads characters it has just written, which is why run-length is a special case. A step button and a "copy as a block" toggle show the difference between the right and the wrong implementation.
- **Scene 3 · Canonical codes.** Edit the **code lengths** of a small alphabet (steppers). A **Kraft** readout `sum 2^-length` says *oversubscribed* (above 1: not a prefix code), *complete* (exactly 1) or *incomplete*. The lesson assigns codes by the DEFLATE rule (shorter codes first, equal lengths in symbol order, counting up): the RFC's example lengths `3,3,3,3,3,2,4,4` for `A..H` give `F = 00`, `A = 010`, `B = 011`, `C = 100`, `D = 101`, `E = 110`, `G = 1110`, `H = 1111` (author-run; **verify against RFC 1951 section 3.2.2**). Only the lengths need sending: why DEFLATE ships lengths, not trees.
- **Scene 4 · A real DEFLATE block.** The tokens become bits with the **fixed Huffman code** (literals 0-143: 8 bits, 144-255: 9, end of block and lengths 256-279: 7, 280-287: 8; five-bit distance codes; the length and distance extra-bit tables) in a `BitStrip` / `ByteGrid`, packed least-significant-bit first. The output is a **valid raw DEFLATE stream**, so the real decompressor can read it: a readout calls the browser's `DecompressionStream('deflate-raw')` on the lesson's own bytes and shows the text it returns (feature-detected: where the browser lacks it the readout says so and nothing else changes; the Node test is the guarantee). `aaaaaaaaaa` is **30 bits (4 bytes)**, `abracadabra abracadabra` is 102 bits (13 bytes), `ab` repeated 50 times is 3 tokens and **42 bits (6 bytes)** against 100 bits (12.5 bytes) for its order-0 entropy bound (all author-run). The lesson shows the real compressor's size beside it (`CompressionStream('deflate-raw')`, labelled **real**), and they are not expected to match: a real compressor picks dynamic blocks and a smarter parse, and for very short inputs either can be smaller. A caption never claims the lesson beats it.
- **Scene 5 · Where each idea works.** LZ77 removes repeats, Huffman removes skew, and `gzip` is DEFLATE plus a header and a checksum (named). `ab` repeated has order-0 entropy of 1 bit per character yet compresses far below it, which the lesson computes and the entropy lesson predicted. Snappy, LZ4 and zstd are named with one line each, **from memory, verify**.
**Out of scope.** Dynamic Huffman block headers (named; the code-length alphabet is not taught), lazy matching and hash chains (named; the lesson parses greedily and says so), `gzip` and `zlib` framing, LZ78 and LZW, `zstd`'s entropy stage.
**Kit.** `core/huffman` (code lengths and **`canonicalCodes`**, the new kit item), `BitStrip` and `ByteGrid` (bit and byte output), `CodePanel` (decoder pseudo-code, `active` line), `ArrayStrip` (the window and lookahead), `Slider`, `Segmented`, `TextField` limits, `ChipButton`, `Stat`, `createRecorder`, `Provenance`.
**AC.**
- **Watch:** the window `--info`; the lookahead and the match being tried `--hi`; a literal `muted`; a back-reference `--violet` with its arrow; bytes saved `--ok`, bytes added by a poor match `--bad`; an oversubscribed code `--bad`, complete `--ok`, incomplete `--orange`; characters outside the window `--dim`.
- **Misconception:** "compression finds the shortest possible description", and "a longer match is always better". A quiz question gives random letters and asks why the output is larger than the input after the headers.
- **Correctness:**
  - **Round trip:** decoding the tokens equals the text for 1,000 seeded strings of every window and minimum-match setting; every match is valid (`distance <= window`, `length <= 258`, characters equal).
  - **Real inflate as the oracle:** the lesson's fixed-Huffman bytes decoded by **`node:zlib` `inflateRawSync`** equal the original text for those 1,000 strings (author-run: 300 of 300 on a prototype), which pins the bit order, the length and distance tables and the end-of-block code.

**`markov-chains` · Markov Chains** [domain review]
Track `math` · *"Where you go next depends only on where you are"* · Builds on: none (context: `probability-basics`; recommend adding it to the registered prerequisites)
**Spec.**
- **Scene 1 · A weather chain.** Three states (Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy) and a transition matrix the learner edits: changing one probability in a row rescales the rest of that row so it still sums to 1 (shown exactly, not rounded to look right). A token hops the weighted `GraphView` (seeded), a **visit bar chart** of the fraction of time in each state converges, and the true **stationary distribution** `pi` (`pi P = pi`, sums to 1) is drawn as reference lines: here `pi = (38/67, 18/67, 11/67) = (0.5672, 0.2687, 0.1642)` (author-run). A speed control and a *run 100,000 steps* button fill the chart from the seeded generator.
- **Scene 2 · How fast it forgets.** Start at Sunny, Cloudy or Rainy (or any distribution) and step the **distribution** itself: `pi_t = pi_(t-1) P`, with the **total variation distance** to `pi` plotted. For a 3-state chain the eigenvalues are `1` and the two roots of `x^2 - (tr - 1) x + det = 0`, so the decay rate is `|lambda_2|`; the default gives `0.558` and `0.242` (trace 1.8, determinant 0.135). The lesson names the three conditions (**irreducible, aperiodic, finite**) and computes whether the chain on screen has them (communicating classes and the period).
- **Scene 4 · A text chain.** A tiny invented corpus (or up to 60 typed words) becomes a **bigram** chain over words; a seeded walk generates sentences. The lesson shows what the Markov property forgets (everything but the last word) and why the output drifts, and points to PageRank (next) as the random-surfer chain. It does not claim modern language models are first-order Markov chains over words.
**Out of scope.** Hidden Markov models, MCMC, continuous-time chains and queueing models (`queueing-theory`), reinforcement learning's use of Markov decision processes (named), mixing-time theory beyond `lambda_2`.
**Kit.** `GraphView` (weighted directed state graph, `edgeMark` by probability, self-loops), `Plot2D` (`Bars` for visit frequencies, `Curve` for the distance and guide), `Slider` (rows), `ChipButton` (presets), `Segmented` (start state), K3-18 `core/linalg` (`powerIteration` for `pi`, matrix powers) plus `eigen2x2` (the new kit item), `core/random`, `useTickSim` (K3-2) or `useSimulation`, `Stat` (K3-3), `createRecorder`, `Provenance`. - **Misconception:** "a Markov chain remembers its history", and "every chain settles to one distribution". A quiz question gives the periodic chain and asks what the bars do after a million steps.
- **Correctness:**
  - `pi` satisfies `pi P = pi` and sums to 1 (residual below 1e-12); the power-iteration `pi` equals the **direct solve** of `(P^T - I) pi = 0` with the normalisation row (`core/vec` `solve`) on 1,000 seeded irreducible chains.

**Tier 2 fence.** `matrices-transformations`: "eigenvectors stay in T3 `eigen-pagerank`." That lesson drew what a 2 x 2 does; this one asks which directions it only stretches.
**Spec.**

For `[[2, 1], [1, 2]]` (eigenvalues 3 and 1, eigenvectors `(1, 1)` and `(1, -1)`) every arrow swings toward the `(1, 1)` line and the ratio of the second to the first component (in the eigenvector basis) shrinks by exactly `1/3` each step. - **Scene 2 · When nothing lines up.** Presets: **rotation** (no real eigenvectors: arrows keep turning, and the lesson says the eigenvalues are complex), **shear** `[[1, 1], [0, 1]]` (one eigen-direction; arrows approach it only like `1/k`, slowly, not geometrically), **equal stretch** `diag(2, 2)` (every direction is an eigenvector: nothing changes), **flip** `diag(2, -2)` (the line is kept but the arrow alternates). - **Scene 4 · Where it leaks.** A **dead end** (a page with no out-links) and a **spider trap** (a group linking only to itself). A toggle *dead ends: drop their rank / spread it uniformly*. With `d = 1` and dropping, the total rank **shrinks to 0** (the 3-page chain `a -> b -> c` loses all rank in 3 steps, author-run); with a trap and `d = 1`, all rank ends inside the trap; raising the damping above 0 and spreading uniformly restores sums of 1 and a sensible ranking. The total rank is a visible readout.

- **Correctness:**

**Tier 2 fences.** `clustering-kmeans`: "dimensionality reduction stays in T3 `svd-pca`." `embeddings` draws a PCA by power iteration kept **local** ("T3 `svd-pca` is the second user"): this lesson uses K3-18 `pca`, and the report files a Phase 2 request for `embeddings` to migrate to it.
